Comparing turn-taking in GPT-Live-1 and gpt-realtime-2.1

I ran OpenAI’s full-duplex GPT-Live-1 through the same turn-taking tests I used on gpt-realtime-2.1. It kept talking through 19 of 20 backchannels and spoke during 54 of 117 mid-sentence pauses. When the caller tried to interrupt, GPT-Live stopped in 14 of 15 trials, with a median stop time of about 1.4 seconds.

Contents

In my last post, I measured how gpt-realtime-2.1 decides that you’ve finished talking. A turn is one stretch of speech before the other side responds. The API uses VAD, or voice activity detection, to decide when that stretch ends.

With the default VAD setting, short pauses often ended the caller’s turn. Semantic VAD cut in less often, but its slowest setting had a 90th-percentile reply wait of 8.7 seconds. A listener’s “yeah” also stopped playback every time in that test.

GPT-Live-1 came out three days before those runs. It’s full duplex, meaning it can listen while it speaks, and its API doesn’t expose the same turn-detection settings. I ran the same 12 sentences, 2 synthetic voices, pause lengths and caller clips against it.

  1. GPT-Live kept talking through 19 of 20 backchannels, the brief “mm-hm” and “yeah” signals that tell a speaker to continue. The gpt-realtime client stopped playback on all 32 backchannel trials.
  2. GPT-Live stopped in 14 of 15 interruption trials. Its median stop time was 1.41 s, compared with about 0.13 s for the gpt-realtime client.
  3. GPT-Live spoke before the caller finished in 54 of 117 pause trials, more than any gpt-realtime setting. In 12 of those, it only acknowledged the caller with words such as “Got it” or “Mm-hmm.”
  4. Among replies with a measured gap, GPT-Live’s median wait was 1.30 s and its slowest was 3.5 s. It gave no reply in 2 of the 117 pause trials.

The two stop times measure different things

Each trial opened a fresh session. The harness sent the caller’s audio in real time in 20 ms frames, then reconstructed playout, the time each returned audio chunk would reach the listener after waiting in the playback queue.

For gpt-realtime, I logged events for the start and end of caller speech and for the response lifecycle. When the server reported that caller speech had started, my client flushed the queued model audio. Its stop time therefore includes server speech detection, network delay and the client flush.

GPT-Live doesn’t emit the same speech-start event or complete-turn markers. I measured its timing from the recorded audio. A GPT-Live stop means that its audio went quiet; the data doesn’t show the model’s internal decision point.

I used the GPT-Live transcript to separate acknowledgments from replies. If everything it said before the caller finished was an acknowledgment such as “okay,” “got it,” “mm-hmm,” “go on” or “sure,” I counted it as an acknowledgment. Anything else counted as a reply.

This is a blunt classification rule. The data table under each chart shows the counts so the split is inspectable.

GPT-Live spoke during 54 of 117 paused sentences

For the pause sweep, the caller said the first half of a sentence, paused for 0.3 to 2.5 seconds, then continued. The chart shows how often audio from each model reached the listener before the caller finished.

During dictation, GPT-Live spoke early in 18 of 29 trials. Eight were acknowledgments. The other 10 were replies, most of which read back part of the number, as in “Got it, 4771.”

GPT-Live also spoke after a first half that already sounded complete, such as “My order hasn’t arrived yet.” It acknowledged the caller in 3 of 30 trials and started a reply in 20. The semantic VAD settings started replies in 8 or 9 of about 30 comparable trials.

On unfinished clauses, GPT-Live spoke early in 13 of 58 trials. Server VAD did so in 17 of 56, while each semantic VAD setting did so in only 1 or 2 trials. One GPT-Live case was an acknowledgment; the other 12 were replies.

I didn’t expect the replies to unfinished clauses. After “How long should I boil an egg if I want the yolk,” GPT-Live answered “For a runny yolk, about six minutes” before the caller said “still a little runny?” In another trial, it agreed to set a reminder before the caller had said when.

Those guesses can be right. When they aren’t, the caller has to talk over an answer to a question they haven’t finished asking.

Longer pauses made this more likely. GPT-Live spoke early in 1 of 22 trials at 0.3 s, 8 of 23 at 0.6 s (6 of those were just acknowledgments), 12 of 24 at 0.9 s, 17 of 24 at 1.5 s and 16 of 24 at 2.5 s.

GPT-Live’s measured reply gaps topped out at 3.5 seconds

The pause test also measured the silence between the caller’s final word and the first audible reply. This is a different measurement from speaking during the pause.

The chart contains 114 measured GPT-Live gaps. Their median was 1.30 s and their 90th percentile was 1.83 s. Server VAD measured 1.38 s and 1.82 s.

GPT-Live’s slowest measured reply took 3.5 s, while 2 of its 117 pause trials produced no reply. In one more trial it answered during the pause and said nothing after the caller finished, so there was no gap to measure.

Semantic VAD on low had a 90th percentile of 8.7 s. In the previous test, the intact capital-of-Australia question got a reply in about 1.5 s. A pause before “Australia is?” pushed the wait to about 9 s, close to the result for sending that fragment alone.

Time from the caller’s last word to the first audible reply, one trial per cell. Rows for GPT-Live are highlighted.

For the capital question, GPT-Live answered the intact, split and fragment-only versions in 1.1 to 1.7 s across both voices. I didn’t see the long fragment-related delay from semantic VAD in these trials. That’s an observation about the outputs, not evidence of how GPT-Live decides to respond.

GPT-Live ignored 19 of 20 backchannels and took 1.41 seconds to stop

I asked each model for a long wifi troubleshooting walkthrough. Then, 2.5 s after its audio became audible, the caller played one of seven clips. Four were backchannels that meant “keep going”; three were attempts to take the floor.

For stopping, the gpt-realtime client treated every clip the same way. It flushed playback after the server reported speech, and the audio stopped in 85 to 189 ms whether the caller said “mm-hm” or “wait, stop.”

GPT-Live kept talking through 19 of 20 backchannels. It stopped once, after an “okay,” with 0.96 s of overlap. For interruptions, it stopped in 14 of 15 trials, with a median of 1.41 s and a range of 0.66 to 2.12 s.

In the remaining interruption trial, it kept talking through “Wait, stop.” The recorded example below shows another “Wait, stop” trial in which it finished the current phrase and went quiet after 1.5 s.

So this isn’t a like-for-like comparison. gpt-realtime’s fast result came from my client flushing playback on a speech-start event. A client could wait for more evidence before flushing, which may help with backchannels but would also delay some interruptions.

GPT-Live has no setting for this, so there’s nothing on the client side to tune. Once it stopped, it replied to the interruption in all 14 stopping trials.

In one trial, the caller asked “what if I don’t know the router password?” GPT-Live answered, “No problem, you don’t need it for these steps,” and continued.

Measurement notes

The network outage changed the run, not the final sample

My connection dropped during the GPT-Live pause sweep, and 61 trials failed to connect. I reran all of them. One trial was still in flight when I stopped the run, and I excluded 2 in which the harness fell behind real time, leaving 117 of the planned 120.

I had to fix the stop detector

My first detector expected model audio to end cleanly. GPT-Live often produced a short trailing fragment before going quiet, so the detector missed some stops and labeled them “kept talking.”

I changed the detector to accept that trailing fragment and rescored every saved trial for both systems. None of the gpt-realtime results changed.

I aligned transcript words to the audio

GPT-Live’s transcript timestamps ran about 0.9 s ahead of its audio (median across 115 trials). To classify what it said before the caller finished, I shifted each trial’s transcript so its first word matched the first model audio.

What these measurements mean for a voice app

  • For instructions and walkthroughs, GPT-Live kept speaking through 19 of 20 backchannels. The tested gpt-realtime client stopped on every one.
  • For callers who need to cut in quickly, GPT-Live’s median stop time was 1.41 s. Test whether that amount of overlap works for your users.
  • For dictation and forms, GPT-Live spoke during 18 of 29 pauses. Eight were acknowledgments and 10 were replies.
  • GPT-Live’s slowest measured reply gap was 3.5 s, though 2 of 117 pause trials got no reply. Semantic VAD stayed quieter during unfinished clauses but had longer waits.
  • gpt-realtime exposes turn and response events. GPT-Live doesn’t expose the same turn markers, so I had to debug these trials from audio and transcript.

One model version each, synthetic caller voices, one client network, September 2026. About $2.40 of GPT-Live sessions for this post. Hosted models change; treat this as a dated measurement.
