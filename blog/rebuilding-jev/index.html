Rebuilding Jev

TypeSafe shipped a model that answers questions about text without generating any text. The first half of this is what Jev is and what its makers do and do not tell you. The second half is a small one I built to check whether the parts that sounded clever actually matter. Two of my four guesses were wrong.

Sahasra Ranjan ~8 min read 206K parameters, trained from scratch · CPU only · code
Contents

What Jev does

TypeSafe AI released Jev last week. You give it a document and a list of questions whose possible answers are fixed in advance. It returns one answer per question, each with a probability.

It never writes a sentence. There is no token-by-token generation, so a call takes one pass through the model instead of dozens. That is why they can charge for input and give output away free. There is no output to charge for.

No paper came with it. No parameter count, no training details, and no calibration numbers, which is odd, because calibrated probabilities are the whole pitch.

Three kinds of question

You do not describe what you want in a sentence and hope. You declare questions, and every question is one of exactly three shapes.

TypeYou give itYou get back
yes / noa questiona single probability
choiceup to 255 optionsthe pick, plus how the probability is spread over the rest
scorean ordered scale, 2 to 10 stepsa weighted average across the scale

The yes/no type is the one that gives the design away. It returns a probability rather than a boolean, so the threshold lives in your code rather than in the model. You decide what counts as confident enough.

The score type is quietly clever too. It is not a number the model writes down. It is the average across an ordered scale, weighted by how likely each step is, which is why it moves smoothly instead of clustering on round numbers the way a model asked to "rate this 1 to 10" tends to.

What actually changed inside the model

The body of the model is an ordinary transformer: embeddings, then a stack of layers. Nothing there is new.

The change is the last layer. A language model ends with a layer that scores every word in the vocabulary, picks one, appends it, and runs the whole model again for the next word. That final layer is enormous: for Qwen3-0.6B it is 1,024 × 151,936, about 156 million numbers, and roughly a quarter of the entire model.

Delete it and put a layer that produces one score per option in its place. That layer is 1,025 numbers. The generation loop goes with it, because there is no next word to guess.

This is also where the type guarantee comes from, and it isn’t learned. The scores are turned into probabilities that sum to 1 across the options you listed. There is no leftover probability to assign to anything else, so an answer outside your list isn’t unlikely. There is nowhere for it to come from.

What they claim

TypeSafe came out of stealth on 15 September with 40 million dollars behind them. The founder, Diogo Almeida, worked on ChatGPT and on RLHF at OpenAI before spending about two years building this.

The headline numbers are 70 to 500 milliseconds end to end, four and a bit cents per million input tokens, and nothing at all for output. They also published a comparison against frontier models on four workflows: security triage, agent-trace review, invoice handling and customer service.

Read that as a price argument rather than a capability one. Jev matches the cheaper frontier model almost exactly and costs about a seventy-sixth as much per decision. It gives up six points to the bigger reasoning models, which cost two to four hundred times more.

One caveat worth holding onto: the "right answers" in that table were taken to be the average of what two frontier models predicted. So accuracy here measures agreement with an ensemble of other models, not agreement with reality. TypeSafe also wrote the four workflows themselves, which they acknowledge.

The claim I find more durable is a different one. Across the same tests, the share of calls that came back structurally unusable was 0% for Jev, 0.58% for OpenAI's smaller models, 5.73% for Opus 5 and 45.5% for Haiku 4.5. That zero is not a score anyone achieved. It is the only value the column can take, for reasons the next section gets into.

What they did not publish

No paper. No parameter count. Nothing about where the training data came from. Their training method has a name, Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, and that is roughly all it has.

The omission that bothers me most is calibration. The whole pitch is that the probabilities mean something, and there is not a single reliability curve, expected calibration error or Brier score anywhere in the launch material. Their own FAQ lists "Is Jev just a smaller LLM?" as a question and then does not answer it.

That is not a scandal. Companies ship products without papers all the time. But it does mean that anyone who wants to know whether this design works has to go and find out.

Where it is weak

To their credit, TypeSafe are direct about this, and the list is coherent. These are all things a single pass with no room to work would be expected to struggle with.

  • Arithmetic and counting. There are no intermediate steps, so there is nowhere to do the arithmetic.
  • Dates. Treated as ordinary text, so "is this older than thirty days" is unreliable. Work it out in your own code and hand over the answer.
  • Chains of reasoning. Accuracy falls away as the number of logical hops between the document and the answer grows.
  • Irrelevant detail. A long document full of noise costs accuracy, and unlike a chat model you cannot instruct your way around it. You curate the input instead.
  • Hostile input. Not hardened against it, which matters given that screening for jailbreaks is one of the pitched use cases.

Writing text is also absent, but that is the design rather than a gap.

The toy I built

That is everything I could learn about Jev from the outside. The rest of this is what happened when I built a small version to check whether the parts that sounded clever actually do anything.

206,081 parameters. Four transformer layers. It trains on a laptop CPU in forty seconds. All of it is here: about 600 lines, no framework beyond PyTorch.

Nothing here is pretrained. The weights start random and only ever see the made-up data below. Where I quote Qwen3-0.6B figures, that is a size comparison to show what the same change costs on a real model. I never trained it, and none of the results come from it.

The training data is made up, and that is the point. I generate each document from a recipe I choose, so I can compute the correct answer’s exact probability with Bayes’ rule.

That gives me something real data never does: a ceiling. On this task the best possible score is 0.687. When my model scores 0.687 it isn’t mediocre, it’s perfect, and the other 31% is the task being genuinely undecidable.

One limitation, stated up front

My documents are bags of words, so shuffling them doesn’t change the right answer. That turns out to break one of the four experiments, and it’s better to know it now than to discover it in the results.

How it was trained

Four steps, and the second one is the unusual part.

The whole run is twenty thousand documents, ten epochs, forty seconds on a laptop CPU. Testing is five thousand held-out documents, and because the recipe is known I can also compute the best score anyone could possibly get, which makes “is this good?” a question with a number rather than a feeling.

Four guesses

Did it work? On the thing that matters most, yes. The model landed exactly on the best score the task allows, and its stated probabilities track reality closely. On flexible answer lists, only partly. And one of my four guesses turned out to be untestable on a task this simple.

Why the mask test told me nothing

Transformers control which words may look at which other words. I expected that choice to matter here: let the document look at itself in both directions but not forward at the options, and the document’s work stops depending on what you asked, so you can reuse it across a dozen questions. That is the design I guessed Jev uses, though TypeSafe has confirmed nothing.

So I trained three models that differed only in which parts of the text could see which other parts. They scored identically.

The reason is in how the input is laid out. The options sit at the end of the text. So even under the most restrictive mask, where each word can only look backwards, the options are last, and looking backwards means looking at the whole document anyway.

One thing did hold. The fully-open mask, where everything sees everything and the reuse trick becomes impossible, scored +0.0002 better. So reusing the document’s work costs nothing.

Why the answer order broke it

I listed the same options in a different order and asked again. More than a third of the answers changed.

This is the result that changed what I’d build. Picking answers from a list you supply at call time is the most attractive thing about this design, and it is much shakier than it looks.

Why honest probabilities need honest targets

Two identical models. One trained on the right answer. One trained on the right answer’s probability.

Training on a plain label throws away the fact that the label was uncertain. When two annotators disagree and you record only the majority vote, you have deleted the most useful thing you had.

One honest caveat: plain labels aren’t broken in principle. With unlimited data both models end up in the same place. The problem shows up with limited data, where a model with room to spare drives its own confidence to 100% and stays there.

New answer lists: right answer, wrong confidence

I trained the model on a four-way question only, then asked it two-way and three-way questions it had never seen.

The label survives. The probability does not, and the probability is the reason you’d build this instead of an ordinary classifier.

Should you build one?

Before answering that I ran the obvious control, because the post kept telling you to use a plain classifier instead without ever measuring one. Same backbone, same loss, same seed. The only changes are that the options leave the input and the head becomes a fixed four-way one.

They are the same model to within noise. I had expected the plain one to be meaningfully quicker, since its input is 14 tokens rather than 18, but at this size the fixed overhead of a forward pass swamps four tokens and the gap is 3%.

So the flexible design buys precisely one thing, and it is the thing measured at 36% order sensitivity with confidence that does not survive an unfamiliar answer list. That is the whole decision:

Is your list of possible answers fixed? yes → use an ordinary classifier. Smaller, faster, simpler. no → keep reading. Do many questions share one long document? yes → the reuse trick is worth real money. Do the answer options change between calls? yes → shuffle options in training, and measure the flip rate. Test calibration on unseen option sets separately. Do you need conclusions about masks that hold on real text? → use a task where word order matters. Mine didn’t.

What this doesn’t show

This is a 206,000-parameter model on invented data. It doesn’t tell you how Jev is actually built. TypeSafe has confirmed nothing, and my mask inference could be wrong. The two failures are failures in my setup, and a bigger model on real text might behave differently.

What it does establish is narrower and still useful. Training on probabilities rather than labels reaches the best score the task allows and stays honest about its confidence, across every difficulty I tried. Answer lists that change between calls work for the answer and not for the confidence. And a clever attention mask, on a task like this one, buys nothing over the plain one.

Finding that out cost twenty cents.

The obvious next step is the one I cannot run yet. All of this is a tiny model on invented words, and the interesting version is a real one on real text: a few hundred million parameters, actual support tickets and moderation data, and the same four questions asked again. That needs a GPU, and my cloud quota request for one is still sitting in a queue. When it clears I will write up what changes.
